The Supply Chain Signal

Issue 01

A short newsletter for supply chain and ops leaders who want to get ahead of problems instead of firefighting them.

In this issue: how supply chain leaders can catch a late order before it's late, and where the handoffs between planning, the warehouse, and suppliers cost you OTIF.

Everyone knows an OTIF miss usually starts upstream. The more useful question is: what signals can we use to spot an issue coming in time to act? The thing I keep seeing with supply chain teams is that the first risk signal shows up way before the miss, on a record nobody's watching.

Getting ahead of late orders isn't about a better delivery dashboard. It's about catching that signal while you still have options.

Here's the approach I share with leaders that makes it possible to tune into the signals that matter most, and the complications to watch out for along the way.

The approach

I see this play out the same way almost every time, so let's walk through one order.

An order for a top account is due to ship a week from this Friday. It needs one purchased component. The supplier confirmed, within their standard 48-hour SLA, a receipt date of the Tuesday before ship day. That leaves three working days of buffer before the truck needs to leave.

1

Decide what "at risk" means before the order is late.

Tuesday comes and goes. No receipt. It's now Thursday, two days late against the confirmed date, and one day before Friday's 16:00 carrier cutoff. That's the first risk signal, a full day before anything on a report says "late." Your team can already rattle off several signals like this: a late supplier receipt on a needed component, a promise date changed after confirmation, a pick that won't make the carrier cutoff.

The signals worth acting on are almost always a combination of things: an event, its timing relative to the order, and what else is already going on with that order.

2

Connect the events that carry the signal.

By Thursday afternoon, the missing receipt is a hole in the inventory allocation. The pick can't happen. The purchase order, the supplier confirmation, the allocation, and the pick each sit in a different system. None of them says "Friday's shipment is at risk" on its own. The signal only appears when you can see them as one timeline for that order.

3

Watch it while there's still time to act.

Seen this Thursday morning, ahead of the 16:00 cutoff, and someone can expedite the part, substitute a lot, or reset the promise before the customer finds out the hard way. Wait for the month-end OTIF review, and the same information just explains what already went wrong. The risk needs to get surfaced for the people on your team who can take proactive action before it becomes a consequence.

Challenges to watch for

The question won't hold still.

Today it's late orders, next week it's a supplier, next month a plant. If each new question means rebuilding the data pipeline all over again, momentum stalls and ROI is questioned. What you want is a reusable model of how your operation runs, one you extend instead of rebuild every time the question changes.

You have to be able to see the logic.

If "at risk" is defined inside one person's SQL code running behind the scenes, your team can't see how the call was made, can't challenge it, and won't act on it. Trust is vital, and people don't move on a number they can't interrogate. The logic that flags risk has to be visible and inspectable, or it won't change what anyone does on Monday.

There's never just one failure mode.

There are dozens, and they shift week to week. A fixed checklist catches the problems you already thought of and misses the ones you didn't. To stay ahead, your system needs to keep surfacing new patterns as they emerge. I worked with a team that used a static list updated at a quarterly review. It just wasn't able to keep up with the business.

AI can be confidently… wrong.

AI helps teams go broader, deeper, and faster than anyone working by hand. But large language models like OpenAI's GPT, Anthropic's Claude, and Google's Gemini are non-deterministic: ask the same question twice and you can get a different answer each time. The bigger risk is that when these tools don't know something, they don't stop. They produce a confident, plausible answer anyway: a hallucination.

I've seen a single confidently wrong AI answer in an ops review poison trust in the whole thing.
